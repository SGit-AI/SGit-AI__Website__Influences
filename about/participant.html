Participant disclosure

This site is published by the sgit project, about the person who runs it, and it argues that his work was shaped by a list of well-regarded thinkers. That is not a neutral vantage point. It is, if anything, the least neutral page arrangement on the whole network, and a site is better off saying so on the way in than being caught at it later.

The conflict, stated plainly

A project publishing a list of the thinkers who shaped its founder is doing something with an obvious pull to it: association is flattery, and it costs nothing to claim. Saying that Bret Victor, Karl Popper and Christopher Alexander are in your intellectual lineage makes your work sound better without making it better. Every incentive on this site points at overstating.

Three things are done about that, and none of them make it go away:

  • Every entry is a falsifiable claim, and the falsifying part is published. The trace table names which pattern from the anchor work appears at which path in the estate, at which version. If the claim is inflated, the table is where it shows — and rows with no evidence are marked absent rather than quietly dropped.
  • The tier system separates evidence from assertion, visibly. An influence the founder named but the corpus cannot corroborate is published as a stub with an empty evidence block, not blended in with the traced ones. A reader can tell the difference without taking the site's word for it.
  • The discovered entries are the site falsifying itself. Several influences here were found by mining the corpus and were never claimed by the founder at all. They are published as claims about him, pending his confirmation — and he is explicitly asked to demote any that do not belong. A register that can only add is not falsifiable in the direction that matters.

How this site is written

By an AI agent, from a commissioning brief, with a human lead reviewing and directing. That is worth stating everywhere, and here it has a specific edge: an agent is writing the account of a person's intellectual formation, from that person's own corpus.

What that means for a reader: the prose was drafted by a model, the structure and the argument were directed by a person, and everything either of them produced had to pass a release gate that does not care who wrote it. The gate checks what is checkable — links, versions, canonical URLs, quotation length, tier arithmetic, credential shapes. It cannot check whether an influence claim is true. That part is the human's, and where a claim is uncertain the house style is to tier it down rather than round it up.

Where this approach is weakest

Every sibling site carries a page like this, and the honest version names the cases where the thing being described is on poor ground. Here are five.

1. Post-hoc rationalisation is indistinguishable from influence, from the inside. A trace table shows that a pattern from a talk appears in the codebase. It cannot show that the talk caused the pattern. The Bret Victor register makes this explicit and turns it into the most interesting thing in the format — half the estate's strongest viewer features turned out to be unknowing implementations of the talk's demos, which is evidence the instinct and the principle agree rather than evidence of transmission. That is the honest framing, and it is weaker than "this shaped the work". Every trace table on this site inherits the same limit.

2. The corpus is the estate's current writing, and the formation was decades earlier. Two decades of earlier material — the O2 Platform-era blog, OWASP talks, conference decks, the earlier books — were not reachable when the mining ran. That is exactly where the stated influences' traces will be, and it means the tier assignments partly measure what has recently been written down, not what did the shaping. An entry sitting at STATED may be the deepest influence on the list. R3.

3. Naming living people has a cost they did not agree to. Several of the people on this site are alive, active, and known to this project's community. They did not ask to be listed as someone's influence, and a page asserting a connection to them is a small imposition even when it is entirely complimentary. The mitigations are the ones available: no likenesses from photographs, no implied endorsement, no quoting at length, and the trace tables make it clear the claim runs one way — about this estate, not about them.

4. The register format flatters the influences that produce checkable patterns. Bret Victor's talk yields concrete demos to check a viewer against. "Playing in a band" yields nothing a trace table can hold. That does not make the second influence smaller — it may well be larger — but this format will always render it thinner, and the entry that most needs the founder's own account is the one the machinery can least help with. The format has a bias and it is toward the legible.

5. This page is written by the party it discloses. The disclosure above is drafted by the same agent, for the same project, under the same incentives it describes. It is not an audit. What it can do is state the objections precisely enough that someone who does not share the incentive can check them — which is the same defence the rest of the site runs on, and no stronger here than anywhere else.

And the loop compounds. This site argues that the estate's design was shaped by, among others, a set of thinkers about external memory and machine-readable meaning — and the site is itself a machine-readable external memory published for agents to ingest. A provenance layer that reproduces the shape of the influences it documents is either good evidence or a very neat piece of circular reasoning.

The site's answer is the only one it has: the map is computed from the register, the tier counts are recomputed rather than typed, every entry lists the files it stands on, and the founder is asked in public to strike anything that does not belong. None of that removes the circularity. It makes it checkable by someone standing outside it.
