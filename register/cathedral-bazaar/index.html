The Cathedral and the Bazaar

stated work stub

The essay's factual history belongs to the open-source sibling site. This entry owns only what it did to the founder — which is exactly the part that is not written down anywhere.

Block 1 The anchor

The Cathedral and the Bazaar — Eric S. Raymond — 1997

↗ the anchor work

Linked, never rehosted. This site explains why the work resonated and traces where it was applied; the work itself stays where its author put it.

Published free by its author and still online at its original address, which is itself part of the argument.

Block 2 The founder's words

No first-person statement about this influence exists in the corpus yet. What follows is the site's reading of the evidence, not the founder's words — and it stays labelled as such until his briefing document arrives.

The deconfliction here is unusually sharp. open-source.sgit.ai carries the essay's history, its influence on the movement and the arguments it started. If this entry repeated any of that it would be duplicating a sibling, so it is deliberately empty of everything except the one thing that sibling cannot hold: what reading it in the late nineties or early two-thousands did to a particular person's idea of how software gets made. That is the founder's to write.

Block 3 The principle

Release early, release often, and let the people using the thing find what is wrong with it — openness as a development method rather than a licensing choice.

Block 7 The wider library

The rest of the work, linked and never rehosted, each item with one line on what it adds. The full union of every entry's Block 7 is at /library/.

  • The Cathedral and the Bazaar (1997) — the essay, free at its original address ↗
  • Homesteading the Noosphere (1998) — the follow-up on ownership and reputation in open-source projects ↗
  • Two Bits — Christopher Kelty (2008) — not by Raymond, and the best critical account of what the essay got right and wrong ↗

Awaiting the briefing document

This entry is a stub, and says so. Named by the founder. The corpus covers the essay's factual history through the open-source material and says nothing about its personal effect — and the personal effect is the whole content of this entry.

The research plan

The founder's briefing, N4. The specific question: what changed after reading it — a decision about releasing something, a change in how work was structured, or a way of arguing for openness that he did not have before. Contemporaneous blog posts from the O2 Platform era are the place to look, and they are out of reach (R3).

Tracked as N4 in the briefing queue.

The corpus evidence

None. This influence is on the founder's own list and the corpus scan found nothing to attach to it. That is the definition of the STATED tier, and it is published rather than hidden: an empty evidence block is the most honest thing this page can show.
